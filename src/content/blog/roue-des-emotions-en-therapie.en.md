The wheel of emotions is a visual aid that helps put a precise word on what you feel. At the centre, a few core emotions; toward the edge, finer and finer shades. Moving from "I feel bad" to "I feel discouraged" or "I feel humiliated" changes how you can work on it.

## What it brings

Naming an emotion accurately, sometimes called *emotional granularity*, is associated with better regulation. The wheel is particularly useful:

- when emotional vocabulary is limited or undifferentiated ("stressed", "annoyed" for everything);
- with adolescents, or people with alexithymia;
- to tell apart close feelings (anger, frustration, resentment; sadness, disappointment, shame);
- ahead of work on thoughts, for example [Beck's columns](/en/blog/colonnes-de-beck-restructuration-cognitive).

## Its variants

Several models coexist: Plutchik's wheel (eight primary emotions, with intensities and combinations), the *Geneva Emotion Wheel*, or the "feelings wheel" popularised by Gloria Willcox, arranged in three concentric circles. These models all hold up: they are maps, to be read as such rather than as measuring instruments. The patient picks a word, and that word stays as it is.

## What Orekio does with it, and what stays with the practitioner

In Orekio, the wheel of emotions is a module the practitioner unlocks when they judge it relevant. Between appointments, the patient uses it to note what they feel in a given situation, with the most accurate word possible. At the next appointment, the practitioner finds these entries as they are.

Orekio **shows back** these entries: the patient's words, and the context they noted. The mood summary, the alert and the interpreted curve belong to the practitioner. As with the other modules, this is consistent with its status as a digital care journal, outside the scope of medical devices (see [About](/en/a-propos)).

## In practice

The wheel works well for short, repeated use: a few entries a week, rather than a detailed journal. It is often unlocked together with the [mood-tracking](/en/fonctionnalites) module and, for high-intensity moments, with the [safety plan](/en/blog/plan-de-securite-comment-le-construire) and grounding techniques.
